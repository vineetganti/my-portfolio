Prompt: Think keenly on all of the apps or platforms present today similar to blackberry messenger and give me a summary of how BBM did actually work.

Answer: Here’s how BBM actually worked under the hood, and then a look at how today’s messengers compare.

**How BlackBerry Messenger (BBM) worked**

BBM was built around a single core idea: instead of using phone numbers or email addresses to identify users, everyone got a **PIN** — a unique 8-character hexadecimal code tied to the device, not a SIM card. Adding a contact meant exchanging PINs, which gave BBM a built-in sense of privacy and exclusivity.

Messages were routed through BlackBerry’s own relay infrastructure, called the **BlackBerry Infrastructure (BIS/BES)**. Every message you sent traveled from your device, encrypted over a compressed data channel, to BlackBerry’s servers in Waterloo, Canada, and then out to the recipient’s device. This is why BBM was remarkably efficient even on 2G networks — BlackBerry compressed data aggressively.

The crown jewel was the **delivery and read receipt system** — the iconic D (delivered) and R (read) markers. This was genuinely novel in 2005 and the entire messaging world eventually copied it. You could see the exact moment your message hit the recipient’s inbox and the exact moment they opened it.

BBM also introduced **typing indicators** (seeing the three dots appear), group chats, and broadcast messages — features we now take for granted on every platform.

The weakness was that the whole system was tightly coupled to BlackBerry hardware. When BlackBerry’s market share collapsed between 2012 and 2014, BBM tried going cross-platform on iOS and Android, but by then iMessage and WhatsApp had already filled the gap.

**The BBM legacy in one line**: every feature in that table’s “Key BBM DNA” column that says “receipts,” “typing,” or “group chat” was either directly borrowed from or pushed into existence by BBM. WhatsApp’s double blue ticks, iMessage’s “Read 9:41 AM,” Signal’s minimal metadata philosophy — BBM planted those seeds.

The biggest structural difference across all modern platforms is **where encryption lives**. BBM encrypted in transit through BlackBerry’s own servers (meaning BlackBerry could theoretically read your messages). Signal and WhatsApp (post-2016) encrypt end-to-end, so even the server operator sees only ciphertext. WeChat and Discord still sit closer to the BBM model — encrypted in transit but accessible to the platform. Signal sits furthest from it, treating even metadata as sensitive. iMessage is somewhere in between — true E2E between Apple devices, but falls back to unencrypted SMS when the other person isn’t on Apple.

Prompt: Which of these messaging apps would be closest to being as secure as BBM? Or has the tech surpassed in BBM strictly security and privacy wise? What would you say would be the missing piece if there will ever be a need for something as tightly coupled as BBM?

Answer: **Has the tech surpassed BBM in security?**

Absolutely, and it’s not close. BBM’s security was *tight* for its time but had a fundamental ceiling: BlackBerry held the keys. Governments (India, UAE, Saudi Arabia, the UK) literally threatened to ban BlackBerry unless they handed over access to BBM traffic — and BlackBerry complied in several cases. So BBM was secure against *other users* and casual interception, but not against the platform operator itself.

Modern Signal-Protocol-based apps broke that ceiling entirely. The server mathematically *cannot* read your messages even if it wants to. That’s a qualitative leap, not just an improvement.

**Which app comes closest to BBM’s security philosophy today?**

The honest ranking:

![messaging_security_spectrum](/images/essays/ideal-chat-app/messaging_security_spectrum.png)

**Signal** is the clear winner, and the closest spiritual successor — but it actually goes far beyond what BBM ever achieved. The key difference is philosophical: BBM asked you to *trust BlackBerry*. Signal is architected so you *don’t need to* trust Signal.

**1. Verified identity without a phone number**

BBM’s PIN system was elegant — you knew exactly who you were talking to without handing over your phone number. Signal now has usernames, but the broader ecosystem still largely revolves around phone numbers as identity anchors. A future platform that uses cryptographic keypairs (think passkeys, but for messaging identity) stored on-device — with no central directory — would be genuinely new ground. Your identity would be mathematically provable and phone-number-free.

**2. Decentralization with usability**

Matrix (the protocol powering the Element app) is trying to do this — federated servers, no single point of failure or control. But it’s rough around the edges and fragmented. The missing piece is a protocol that achieves BBM-level smoothness and reliability on a decentralized backbone. Nobody has cracked that UX problem yet at scale.

**3. Hardware-bound trust**

BBM’s PIN was tied to the device, not an account in the cloud. Modern phones have **Secure Enclaves** (Apple) and **Titan chips** (Google) that can hold cryptographic keys that never leave the hardware. A messaging app that roots its identity entirely in the Secure Enclave — making keys physically unextractable — would combine BBM’s device-bound philosophy with modern cryptography. No server, no cloud backup, no fallback. Your device *is* your identity. If you lose it, you regenerate. That’s the trade-off nobody has been willing to force users into because it’s commercially uncomfortable — but it would be the most secure messaging system ever built for consumers.

The honest summary: Signal has beaten BBM on every technical dimension. The gap that remains isn’t cryptographic — it’s about identity, decentralization, and the courage to remove the fallback mechanisms that make people feel safe but quietly become the biggest vulnerabilities.

Prompt: How would be broadly go about in building something that encompasses the architecture of Signal with BBM PIN methodology without coupling it to hardware and also not having to give away user info like phone number or email in accounts?

Answer: The core insight is: BBM used a PIN as a *pointer* to a device. We want a PIN that’s a pointer to a *cryptographic keypair* — one that lives with the user, not on any server.

Let’s break this into three layers — identity, messaging, and transport — because getting all three right simultaneously is what makes this genuinely hard.

**Layer 1 — Identity: the PIN reinvented**

The fundamental problem is: how do you give someone a short, shareable handle that points to a cryptographic identity, without any server knowing who that person is?

The answer is to generate a keypair locally on first launch. The public key becomes the source of truth. From that public key, you derive a PIN — something like a base-32 encoded hash of the first 40 bits of the public key — so it’s short (8 characters, like BBM), human-readable, and mathematically tied to the key. Nobody assigns it. No server registers it. It just falls out of math.

The private key never leaves the device, but it can be exported as an encrypted backup phrase (12-24 words, like a crypto wallet seed) so the user can restore their identity on a new device without coupling to hardware.

![identity_generation_flow](/images/essays/ideal-chat-app/identity_generation_flow.png)

**Layer 2 — Messaging: Signal’s protocol underneath**

Once two parties have each other’s public keys, the actual message encryption is straightforward to specify — you use the Double Ratchet Algorithm exactly as Signal does. Each message gets a fresh encryption key derived from a ratcheting chain, so even if one key is compromised, past and future messages stay protected. This layer is essentially solved technology. You don’t reinvent it; you adopt it.

What you add on top is the BBM-style receipt system, but implemented without the server ever seeing who is messaging whom.

![message_send_flow](/images/essays/ideal-chat-app/message_send_flow.png)

Layer 3 — Transport: the hardest unsolved piece

This is where most systems fail. The relay server above is still a central choke point — it knows which IP addresses are talking to it, even if it can’t read the messages. The full stack for solving this looks like:

A federated relay network (like Matrix) so no single company controls the infrastructure
Onion routing for the transport layer (like Tor, but lighter) so the relay doesn’t even know the IP of who’s sending
Rate-limiting and spam prevention without identity, done via anonymous proof-of-work tokens (similar to how zcash handles shielded transactions)

The last problem — spam and abuse prevention without any identity — is the genuinely unsolved design challenge. BBM solved it by coupling to hardware (a spammer needed physical devices). Signal solves it by requiring a phone number. Remove both, and you need a third mechanism. The current best answer is cryptographic rate-limiting: each PIN gets a quota of tokens that regenerate slowly, and sending a message burns a token. Spam at scale becomes computationally expensive. But this is still an active research area, not a production-ready solution.

The practical build order

If you were actually building this, you wouldn’t tackle all four layers at once. The realistic sequence is:

Start with layers 1 and 2 — keygen, PIN derivation, and the Double Ratchet on top of a conventional (but minimal) central relay. This alone gives you something better than Signal in terms of privacy ergonomics, because there’s no phone number or email anywhere in the flow. The relay at this stage knows IP addresses but nothing else — that’s acceptable for an early product.

Layer 3 (federated relays + onion routing) is an engineering project that adds significant latency and complexity. You’d introduce it in stages — federation first so no single company is the chokepoint, transport anonymity later when you have the user base to justify the infrastructure.

Layer 4 (anonymous anti-spam) is where you’d want to collaborate with cryptography researchers, because no consumer messaging app has cracked this cleanly yet. The proof-of-work token approach is workable but burns battery. Zero-knowledge proof based rate limiting (where you prove “I haven’t sent more than N messages this hour” without revealing how many you’ve sent) is theoretically cleaner but computationally heavier still.

The honest summary of where the frontier sits: layers 1, 2, and the beginning of layer 3 are buildable today with existing open-source primitives — libsodium for crypto, the Signal Protocol library for the ratchet, Matrix’s Synapse for federation reference. Layer 4 remains the genuinely open research problem that separates “very private messaging app” from “fully anonymous messaging system.”
